# Story and delivery

Story is the organization of evidence into an audience's path from current understanding to a decision. It is not decoration added after a chart.

## Build the story

### Beginning

Establish the setting, audience, current state, desired state, and meaningful imbalance. Make the audience the protagonist: the story should concern what they need to understand or do, not what the analyst did.

### Middle

Use only evidence that advances the problem. Include relevant background, comparisons, consequences of inaction, alternatives, supported drivers, and the advantages or tradeoffs of a recommendation.

### End

Resolve the opening question. State the recommendation, action request, decision, or remaining uncertainty. Do not stop at a statistic when the communication exists to support a choice.

The three-part shape is a useful structure, not a compulsory dramatic formula. Do not invent conflict, emotion, or causality.

## Narrative order

Time order is natural but not mandatory. A trusted audience may benefit from the recommendation first and evidence second. Choose the sequence that reduces effort for this audience.

- **Horizontal logic:** read titles alone. They should form a coherent argument or story.
- **Vertical logic:** within each view, title, text, chart, labels, and annotations must support the same conclusion.
- **Reverse storyboard:** after drafting, write each view's takeaway back into an outline; add, delete, or reorder where it differs from the intended story.

Use concise repetition in three roles: preview the message, show the evidence, recap the decision. Do not mechanically duplicate paragraphs.

## Oral versus written delivery

### Live presentation

- Keep the screen simpler because speech supplies context.
- Control sequence and pace.
- Anticipate questions and prepare supporting detail outside the main path.
- Use progressive reveal only when it mirrors the spoken logic.

### Self-contained report or distributed deck

- Include conclusion, context, labels, annotations, definitions, source, and limitations needed without a speaker.
- Permit higher density only when the reading path remains evident.
- Do not ship a speaker-dependent slide as a self-contained report.

When one deliverable must serve both, build a clear visible spine plus subordinate detail that does not compete with live narration.

## Animation

Use animation to reveal time, logic, or a sequence of evidence. Prefer simple appearance, disappearance, transparency, or progressive drawing. Avoid fly-ins, pseudo-3D motion, and effects whose motion becomes the message.

Prepare a static annotated final state for readers who do not see the live sequence. If animation is required to make a confused chart understandable, redesign the static structure first.

## Logical order in categories

Preserve inherent order such as age bands, stages, or rating scales. For unordered categories, order by the story or value. A single dataset can support a positive, negative, or adoption story; title, order, position, color, and annotations must all match the chosen question.

Do not combine several different stories into a single live view when separate views are clearer. A denser synthesis may be acceptable for self-contained reading if it has an obvious path.

## Multi-series and spaghetti charts

Choose by communication need:

- highlight one series and leave others as context for live narration;
- use common-scale small multiples when every series must remain available;
- combine separation and highlight when both context and a focal series are required.

Every small multiple must share the comparison scale. Direct-label series and avoid remote legend lookup.

## Dark backgrounds

Prefer a light background when unconstrained. If a brand or environment requires dark:

- rebuild contrast and palette from the dark base;
- test projection and actual delivery conditions;
- reduce the number of bright colors;
- confirm body text, axes, and context remain readable;
- do not copy a light-theme palette unchanged.

## Acceptance and change

The audience may resist an unfamiliar design even when it improves comparison. Increase acceptance by:

- explaining the practical benefit;
- placing old and new versions side by side;
- offering a small number of meaningful alternatives;
- involving an influential audience member;
- seeking neutral feedback to distinguish habit from a genuine design problem.

## Iteration and sustained practice

- Start with a paper sketch or plain storyboard.
- Create variant A, duplicate it, change one meaningful decision in variant B, and compare.
- Keep rollback points.
- Ask unfamiliar readers what they noticed first, what they think the result is, and what remains unclear.
- Budget time for communication after analysis.
- Keep a library of strong examples with audience, task, encoding, and tradeoff—not just screenshots.

For teams, combine shared fundamentals, one or more internal specialists, and external help when needed. Use real-work redesign sessions and feedback loops; do not outsource a single attractive artifact without transferring reusable knowledge.
